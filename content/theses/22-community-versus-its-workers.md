## Context

Platforms often speak in the language of community, safety and belonging. Those values are also tested internally, through the conditions under which the people building and moderating the service work.

At Grindr, a union campaign and a rapid return-to-office mandate placed the company's public community identity beside a dispute over worker voice, relocation and healthcare continuity.

## Argument

In July 2023 a supermajority of Grindr's staff announced their intention to unionise with the Communications Workers of America. Within weeks the company issued a return-to-office mandate requiring relocation to a hub city, giving workers two weeks to decide. Roughly eighty of 178 employees left; the union noted the burden fell particularly on trans employees, who would have had to find new healthcare providers. On 1 November 2024 the National Labor Relations Board issued a complaint alleging that the policy was retaliation, that the severance agreement presented to workers was unlawful, and that the company had refused to recognise or bargain with the union. Grindr calls the allegations meritless and states that the transition to hybrid work predated the union petition.

The complaint is an allegation awaiting adjudication, and the company disputes its premise. The sequence still matters because it reveals who bears the operational cost of a policy change. A platform designed for a marginalised community cannot separate its social mission entirely from the treatment of marginalised people inside its workforce.
